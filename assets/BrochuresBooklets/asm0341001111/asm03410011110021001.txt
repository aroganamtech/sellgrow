Between 1929 and 1932, local airlines in Mexico, Cuba, Colombia, Peru and Brazil, became associated with the system, adding to the extension of an integrated air transport network. By December 1931, Flying Clippers were flying 21,000 miles of routes in Latin America.
In 1931 Pan American began active groundwork preparations for trans-oceanic service. Routes were charted across the Pacific with service as early as 1935 to Manila. Later, service was extended to Hongkong, Singapore, and Australasia. The first North Atlantic service was initiated in 1939; the South Atlantic was added to the System in the Winter of 1940-41. This long-range ocean flying has been called "the most spectacular feat of American aviation prior to the war.”
In the war years, the story of Pan American has been largely the story of the Air Transport Command and the Naval Air Transport Service, for which Pan American has operated, in addition to its regular commercial flights, helping keep open the vital supply lines to the distant battle fronts—over the oceans to the British Isles, Europe, Africa, India, Australasia, and over the "hump” into China.
Pan American has merged itself with the total war effort.
Here in brief is the unequaled record to the end of 1944: over 80,000 miles of routes in the present System; well over 1,900,000,000 passenger miles flown and 162,000,000 pounds of express and air mail carried via Pan American since 1927.
Cargo and mail gain wings when they go by the Flying Clippers.
