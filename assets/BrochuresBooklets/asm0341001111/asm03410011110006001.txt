fATiN America’s population is separated into dense groups concentrated mainly about a few large seaport cities such as Lima-Callao, Buenos Aires, Santiago-Valparaiso, and Rio de Janeiro. These are, in turn, separated from each other by great distances of undeveloped, and in some cases unexplored, areas; they have served simply as hubs of commerce where raw materials from the adjoining agricultural and mining districts could be shipped abroad in exchange for manufactured articles.
Intra-'La.tm American commerce has been slight-less than 1/1 Oth of these nations’ total export-import trade. Lack of transportation facilities (until the advent of air transport) and cultural differences have discouraged travel and understanding between Latin American countries.
When World War II severed the United Nations from their usual sources of raw materials, they looked toward Latin America with its wealth of foods, hides, wool and cotton, and its minerals such as copper, oil, chrome, antimony and manganese.
New mills and factories, backed by United States dollars plus local capital, have arisen almost overnight to process these materials. Old mines, abandoned because of their remoteness and cartage difficulties, have been re-equipped and are functioning at full speed. New agricultural projects have been initiated.
