i he lands that are Latin America stretch from the Rio Grande to Cape Horn, the southernmost tip of South America—a span of 6,000 miles. In this area of approximately 8,000,000 square miles (lYi times that of the United States) is a population of 130,000,000 people.
Its development has been hampered by great reaches of impassable jungle and high mountain ranges which make impractical an organized system of railroad and highway transportation to link its centers of population and commerce. Railroads must be confined to local business areas, and the majority of long-distance surface traffic moves by pack trains, river boat, ox-cart, or on foot.
Shortly after the close of the first World War,
certain European nations attempted to introduce the airplane as a means of overcoming the difficulties of Latin American terrain, but in October 1927, Pan American World Airways was the first to inaugurate a system of air routes destined to become the only adequate and integrated communication and transportation system for the 20 Latin American nations.
The first hop, a scant 90 miles, reached from Key West to Havana. Now a network of air routes, Pan American routes, connects 300 principal cities in Latin America: 40,000 miles of airways linked to the United States through five international air gateways: Miami, New Orleans, Brownsville, Nuevo Laredo, and Los Angeles.
And four more international gateways to Latin America (New York, Charleston, Tampa, and Houston) are planned by Pan American.
