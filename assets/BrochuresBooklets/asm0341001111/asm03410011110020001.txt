V n October 28, 1927, scarcely 24 years after the Wright brothers lifted their flier into the air over Kittyhawk, a small transport plane raced its engines at Key West airfield and soared into the sky, pointing its nose toward Havana, 90 miles away.
Standing on the field and watching it dwindle to a mere speck in the distance stood a small group of men; it carried with it their faith and confidence in the future of American-flag commercial international aviation. The occasion was the inauguration of the first link in the Pan American Airways System which, within the following 18 years, was destined to serve 68 countries and territories.
With the success of this first enterprise, Pan American expanded with remarkable speed, yet always only after intensive and extensive preparation.
In January 1929, service was extended from Miami to San Juan via Havana, a distance of 1,400 miles. By September this route reached all the way to Paramaribo in Dutch Guiana, an additional 1,400 miles, and by November 1931, to Buenos Aires—4,800 miles away. In February 1929, a service was begun between Brownsville, Texas, and Mexico City, a 470-mile trip, and by September was extended to Guatemala. By May 1930, Clippers were flying this airway to Venezuela.
In January 1929, Pan American Airways entered into agreement with W. R. Grace and Company, operators on the west coast of South America, and Pan American-Grace Airways (Panagra) was formed.
