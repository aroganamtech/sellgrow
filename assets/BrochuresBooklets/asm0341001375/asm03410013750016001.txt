Like any other public seat, Pan Am’s passenger chairs are a compromise. They fit the majority of people rather well. Upright, the seats provide a good support for the way human beings sit. (They also provide maximum protection during a sudden stop; that’s why we require them fully upright for landing and take off.) Reclined, they provide a sort of hammock so that the body can be placed in a position approximating that of a bed. Exactly what position the body assumes is quite individual.
As we said, the seat has been designed to provide the best average platform for its occupant to sit, to read, to eat, or to watch a movie, and at the same time, it has been designed so that its occupant can renounce the alert mode, sitting up, in favor of the relax mode, lying down.
In the latter mode, however, there is an important basic difference between the bedroom and the airliner.
Sleep researchers note that almost everyone asleep on a bed will change positions regularly during the sleep period. There may be other reasons for this, but the big one seems to be that the changes in position force changes in blood flow, so that stale blood is run through the heart and lungs at regular intervals for freshening.
An airline seat is more confining than a bed, of course, so a special routine is called for. If we grant that the unusual position of your body, and the differences between an airplane interior and your bedroom are quite significant, we can anticipate that you’ll wake up from time to time
