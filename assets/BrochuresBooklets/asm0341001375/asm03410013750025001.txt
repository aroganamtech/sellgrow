What happens to you after you leave the plane naturally depends entirely on you and what you’ve got planned. At one extreme, there will be a businessman, arriving for a one-day series of business conferences, followed by a trip back home. The other can be a tourist, arriving for his first day of a three-week trip, say, through Europe.
The businessman may choose not to adjust his clocks at all. He will keep his wristwatch on home time, eat when the hands tell him to, sleep when he can, and arrive at home, tired but requiring little adjustment. He is occupying a personal little time capsule.
For the tourist, the best route may be to move as quickly as possible into the adjustment necessary to operate on the new time. The biological clocks will be upset, but they will adjust, and the completeness of the new routine may help in speeding the change. The main thing to keep in mind is that dysrhythmia is a temporary condition. The exact definition of “temporary” depends on you, your mind, and your body, but another word— “moderation”—can help define it for you.
Slide into your routine as easily as you can. When your brain states plainly that you’re feeling a bit tired, rest a bit. When your stomach announces it’s ready for food, you may want to avoid the depths of a foreign menu—at least at first—and concentrate on something a bit closer to what your system will recognize as nourishment. Like mechanical clocks, biological clocks are adjustable. Both of them require a bit of personal attention to keep them functioning smoothly.
