MAN’S DOMINION OVER DISTANCE—AND PERHAPS TIME
Probably the biggest change in travel patterns has been the price in time and money and energy that men and women have had to pay. Until the jet came along, a traveler simply gave himself up to the length of time it took to get from one place to another. That tortuous 25-day ride from St. Louis to San Francisco via the Butterfield Stage took three weeks and four days, period. Crossing the Atlantic by steamer took about five days (on the average). The Pacific voyages took even longer. A long train trip was, first and last, a long train trip. Even the piston-powered airplane required a major time commitment by its passengers.
When the jet came in, the human mind, an organism noted for its ability to hatch schemes and to delude the person it inhabits, began to think of travel in a different way. Suddenly, and for the first time in recorded history, the amount of travel between two points became incidental to the total travel experience. The time spent in the travel vehicle (the jet) became, for most people, the smallest fraction of the total time away from home.
