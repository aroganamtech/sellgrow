The engineers at Boeing’s 747 plant in Everett, Washington, love to talk about their big new baby. Ask about the 747’s size and, after a momentary pause to collect the figures in the computer bank’s memory, they will start reeling off facts: the 747, they tell you, is the largest airplane ever designed for commercial service. It will have a gross weight of 710,000 pounds —the current family of 707’s weigh 328,000—stretch to a length of 231 feet, four inches and have a wing span of 195 feet, eight inches (those engineers are very particular about inches). Its tail alone will soar 63 feet, five inches above ground, which is the height of an average five-story building. It can carry 490 passengers 45,000 feet into the air at a speed
of 625 miles for 6,000 miles without stopping. The statistics are important but the real story of the 747 is in what this creation of American industrial and aeronautical sciences will mean to the average traveler.
The builders want the traveler to know that the 747 Superjet also will be superior in terms of safety, reliability, payload, speed and economy in comparison to commercial jets now flying. It holds such promise in fact, that following Pan Am's lead, 24 other domestic and foreign airlines have already placed orders with Boeing for 145 of the big birds.
The sheer size of the 747 will enable airlines to get away from what critics have been referring to as ‘‘steerage seating." While the Superjet can carry 490 passengers, the average load will probably be about 360.
