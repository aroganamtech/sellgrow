The most you can hope for is to hail a taxi driver who lives near where you are going. Under the right conditions, which means if your accent is commendable and if the moon is blue, you may be able to talk him into letting you ride in his cab.
Many people think their troubles are over as soon as they are in the back seat. But veterans will tell you your troubles have just begun.
Union rules forbid a driver to do less than 60 miles an hour on straight, crowded streets.
They also forbid his avoiding any engagement that might do damage to another car.
The safest way to ride in a Paris taxi is to lie on the floor of the cab, legs outstretched and arms covering the eyes. If there is more than one person in the taxi, let the heaviest stay on the bottom and the lightest on top.
The seat should be used only for packages and other expendable baggage. The most frightening thing about riding in the back seat is not in seeing what you’re going to hit, but what you’re going to miss.
Panic is the greatest danger a rider faces. More tourists are killed from heart attacks than from automobile accidents.
