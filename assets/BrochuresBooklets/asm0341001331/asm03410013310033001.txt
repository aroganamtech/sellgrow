In 1939 Pan Am turned to the Atlantic. On May 20, the Boeing 314 flyinq boat "Yankee Clipper" took off from Port Washington, New York. This airmail flight landed at Lisbon 26 hours and 54 minutes later, and the next day it continued on to Marseilles. On June 24 airmail service began to Southampton, England. The first transatlantic passenger flight took place on June 28 when the "Dixie Clipper" flew from Port Washington to Horta, Azores and on to Lisbon and Marseilles. Passenger service to England started July 8 on the Port Washington - Shediac, New Brunswick - Botwood, Newfoundland - Foynes, Eire - Southampton route.
Then came World War II. The airline served its government, not only flying priority personnel and cargo, but teaching military pilots the long-range over-water flying and navigation techniques it had developed, by providing the military know-how it had amassed in building stepping-stone air bases across oceans and continents, and by actually operating for the military an air service from the U.S., via South America and across the South Atlantic, across North Africa.
After the war, Pan Am resumed peacetime travel expansion. It turned to commercial use aircraft developed during the war, like the DC-4, first ocean-crossing land plane, the DC-6 and DC-7, the Lockheed Constellation and the Boeing Stratocruiser.
It extended routes even further, and in 1947 the Constellation "Clipper America" inaugurated regularly scheduled round-the-world commercial air service on June 17. The route was New York - Gander - Shannon - London - Istanbul - Dhahran - Karachi - Calcutta -Bangkok - Manila - Shanghai - Tokyo - Guam - Wake -Midway - Honolulu - San Francisco - Chicago - New York, and the Constellation arrived back on June 30.
