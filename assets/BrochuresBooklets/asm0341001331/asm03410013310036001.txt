In the late 1960s, a combination of factors brought financial reverses to the U.S. airline industry and caused Pan Am to experience the first loss years in its history. In seven years -- 1969 through 1975 --Pan Am lost a total of $302.1 million. However, in
1976,	the airline posted a net income of $94.6 million, due largely to a major debenture exchange in which the company realized $102.9 million. In
1977,	the company posted a net income of $45 million, in 1978, a net income of $118 million and in 1979, a net income of $76 million.
Among the negative factors during the 1969-1975 period were excess competition, excess capacity in the early years of the 747, lack of access to domestic markets, yields below those of domestic carriers, and failure of anticipated traffic growth to materialize.
In the early 1970s, as the recession eased, and when the U.S. domestic airlines were returning to profitability with the help of fare increases authorized by the U.S. government, Pan Am, its fares kept down by international pacts and its yields much lower than domestics', imposed stringent cost controls and sought increased efficiency as it worked to return to profitability. The company also designed and put into effect new marketing and service programs. In 1978, Pan Am was permitted to introduce many low budget fares, such as the $999 round-the-world excursion fare. In 1979, Pan Am started daily service on its first domestic route - New York to Los Angeles.
Pan Am's pioneering efforts in the charter flight field resulted in a program that was the largest in the industry during the early 1970s.
