Q. What are some of the factors contributing to poor profits?
A. For one thing, the industry is characterized by excessive competition. Some routes, for instance, have been awarded to more competing companies than the potential traffic justifies. Scheduled airlines have to cope with cut-rate competition from “non-skeds,” or supplemental airlines, that operate only when and where convenient. Airlines compete on the basis of equipment, flight frequencies and services. Scheduled air transportation may be the only regulated industry in the U.S. that consistently earns less than the permitted rate because of excessive competition. Inflation, labor costs rising at a rate exceeding the national average, and other factors have adversely affected profits.
Q. Any other reasons for habitually low profits?
A. A major reason is that airlines have little flexibility to adjust fares rapidly in response to economic conditions. Getting approval of a rate increase or decrease can take many months. Thus, unlike other businessmen, airline managers do not have the option of reacting promptly to, say, a surge in costs or a slump in traffic by raising or lowering prices. The industry operates under rules written for conditions of an earlier year, but still applied in the same way today. Many executives believe a new philosophy of regulation is needed that will permit rapid response to changing economic conditions.
Q. What have the airlines done to offset their losses?
A. They have cut back on expenses. For instance, in the spring of 1971 they were operating 700 fewer daily flights than a year previously. During 1970 they reluctantly furloughed 12,000 employees. To attract additional passengers many airlines instituted promotional fares. Domestic airlines sought a general fare increase, and were granted a 6 percent adjustment in May 1971. These measures to reduce costs and increase revenues are helpful, but may not prove enough to offset heavy inflationary pressures.
