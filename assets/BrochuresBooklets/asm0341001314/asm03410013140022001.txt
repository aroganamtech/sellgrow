Every Flight Is Flown Twice
As your Clipper taxis to the end of the runway, as it lifts smoothly and steadily into the air, you know that over two decades of flying experience are behind the trip that this Clipper is making. But you probably don't know that this flight, and every flight, is “flown” in precise detail, from start to destination, before you board the plane.
Using weather information supplied to them, the Pilot and Flight Dispatcher prepare a Flight Plan. This covers .exact route to be followed, altitude, fuel, speed, and as a final safety measure, designation of at least two alternate airports at destination.
With Clippers in which cabins are pressurized, there is little concern for weather enroute, whatever the season. Pressurization means that the plane can fly at high altitudes, above storms or chance of roughness. This is called “Over-Weather” flying—• smooth, serene, comfortable. Often, weather maps will show that there are favoring winds that can be followed, a further contribution to speed and comfort. Both Pilot and Flight Dispatcher must agree on every detail of the Flight Plan, and before the flight leaves, the entire crew is briefed on details of the Plan.
But this is not all. Using a copy of the Flight Plan as a chart, the flight crew keeps a running record of the flight to be sure that the Plan is strictly adhered to. And once the plane is in the air, the progress of the flight is followed with almost photographic accuracy on the ground. Every hour, or oftener, plane and ground Flight Control stations are in Radiotelephone contact—these constant reports are recorded on plotting boards, so that at all times the exact position of every Clipper is known to Pan American's widespread ground stations. Every minute of the day and night 65 Clippers are in the air. The Clipper on which you fly is not a thing in itself—it is a part of a great and dependable world system.
