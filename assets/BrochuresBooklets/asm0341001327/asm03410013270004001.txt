It was in the Caribbean where experiments in plane-to-shore communications were developed . . . over water-navigation perfected and weather stations set up. Gaining valuable experience in overseas operation, Pan American . . . like a proud eagle wanting to spread its wings and seek new horizons, began to expand its routes in Latin America. But new routes demanded new airports . . . bigger and better planes with a range far beyond that of the Fokker F-7 Pan American engineers helped develop the Sikorsky S-38 amphibian-a pioneer twin-engine aircraft with a range of nearly 300 miles — which literally served as a flying laboratory, supplying the vital information that makes today’s non-stop crossing of the Atlantic commonplace.
The Caribbean was just a start. By 1928, Pan American had blazed a trail to the cities of the South American continent, despite competition from subsidized and entrenched airlines. With 118 employees and seven planes in operation, Pan Am had racked up 297,000 passenger miles and carried 270,000 pounds of mail.
While expanding its routes and service throughout South America, Pan Am was looking forward to the day when an aircraft would be able to conquer the two great oceans. While Pan Am engineers were drawing the specifications for an airplane that would be bigger and better than any the world had 'known, a handful of brave men were exploring the weather and landing conditions that would have to be met by planes flying the Atlantic and Pacific.
