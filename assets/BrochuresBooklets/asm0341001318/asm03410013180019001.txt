Pan Am’s new terminal at Idlewild was opened in May, 1959. And their new 707s began to take over the company’s Pacific routes later in that year, reaching Bangkok and Sydney before the end of 1959.
Pan Am’s Boeing Stratocruisers were being steadily withdrawn from service as the B-707s came on the line. In August, 1960, fourteen Stratocruisers were sold for scrap for the total sum of $105,000! DC-6Bs by then had taken over the short haul operations, replacing the Convair 240s in the Caribbean and Central American services, and at the same time replacing DC-4s in Pan American’s Internal German Service (IGS). Costing $1,200,000 each, the DC-6Bs * proved to be the greatest money-makers of the piston engine era.
Early in 1960 the first of 19 DC-8s was delivered. Although not the money maker which the 707s proved to be, the DC-8s were operated along side the 707s on the Atlantic, and as the exclusive equipment on the African service. Toward the end of the 1960s Pan American gradually sold off their DC-8s, the final one being sold in 1971.
DC-7s continued operating economy class services over the N, Atlantic routes until 1962 at a slightly discounted fare, $20 less than the standard jet fare. As these planes were displaced by jets, a number of them were converted to' all-cargo DC-7F aircraft. These planes carried up to 32,000 lbs. of cargo nonstop from London to New York, using the new pallet loading system developed by Pan American.
B-707 fan jets began to appear on the airline’s routes in 1962; and by the end of that year some 60 jet aircraft had been delivered to Pan Am. Across the Atlantic in mid 1964 a total of 214 jet flights a week were being operated. In 1963 the airline introduced all-cargo versions of the intercontinental 707 fan jets. These
planes carried payloads of up to 76,400 lbs.
On Pan Am’s IGS in the Summer of 1964 some 650 flights per week were being flown by the company’s DC-6Bs between Berlin’s Tem-plehof Airport and the major cities of West Germany. The company was then carrying 1 1/2 million passengers a year on these routes within W. Germany, about two-thirds of the total traffic. BEA and Air France operated in competition with Pan Am on these routes.
Boeing B-727, 1965
The Boeing 727 tri-jets were put into service in 1966, both on Pan American’s Caribbean routes and in the IGS. These 128 passenger jets were capable of operating off the short runways of Templehof Airport, Berlin, bringing Hanover to within 35 minutes of this former capitol of Germany.
1966 was the year in which Pan American and the W.R. Grace Co. sold their jointly owned airline, Panagra, to Braniff Airways at an agreed price of $30 million, all operations and assets of Panagra being taken over by Braniff in February, 1967.
In 1966 Pan Am was operating 20 of their big jets under contract to the Military Airlift Command carrying essential supplies and personnel to Vietnam. Operating DC-6Bs from a Hong Kong base, the company provided a rest and rehabilitation (R&R) service for U.S. servicemen in Vietnam at a one-dollar-a-month charge to the U.S. Government! After the initial 4 months of operations, carrying servicemen to rest leave in Hong Kong, Tokyo and other S.E. Asian cities, the service was provided on a cost basis, without profit to Pan Am! In addition to the 13 DC-6Bs operated on this service there were two 707s which provided daily R&R flights to Honolulu. It is estimated that the first four months of the R&R service, for which Pan American received payment of $4, actually cost the airline $2.8 million!
It was in 1966, too, that Juan Trippe and the Boeing Co. dropped the second shoe, so to speak, in the heady atmosphere of the jet era.
