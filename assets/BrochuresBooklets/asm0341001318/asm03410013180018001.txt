The DC-7C featured an added 10-ft. center wing section which increased fuel capacity to 6,500 gallons, insuring near 100 percent nonstop schedules between the U.S. and Europe. Pan American acquired 26 of this type. Its stretched fuselage (4 ft. longer than the 7B model) gave it a capacity of 92 passengers in the tourist configuration. The plane was used, too, to inaugurate service on the so-called “polar great circle route” from the U.S. west coast to Europe in September, 1957. Non-stop time from San Francisco to London was 18 hours.

The turboprop airliners made a brief flash in the international skies; but Juan Trippe determined to side-step this intermediate technology and take a giant step into the jet transport era! Boeing’s prototype 707 had flown in July, 1954. On October 13, 1955, Mr. Trippe shook up the entire world of air transportation by announcing Pan American’s order for 20 Boeing 707s and 25 Douglas DC-8s! These revolutionary new air transports would cut flying times in half between the principal cities of the world!

By the Spring of 1954 the majority of Pan American’s fleet of Connies had been withdrawn from service and sold. And in July, 1955, the final 3 of the airline’s DC-3s were taken out of scheduled service. In 1955 Pan American’s share of the N. Atlantic traffic had increased to 33 percent of the total. The proportion of the company’s traffic flying tourist class had reached 62 percent system-wide.
The year 1957 was the first year in which the number of passengers flown across the Atlantic exceeded the number travelling by surface vessels.
In the latter half of the 1950s solid evidence was mounting which pointed to the eventual obsolescence of the piston engined airliners on the international air routes. The Comet experience of BOAC, although marred by two catastrophies early in 1954, was an early harbinger of what must eventually come! If ever there was a question of Pan American’s preeminence in the air transport world it was decisively reaffirmed with this momentous announcement! The electric reaction among the world’s airlines is typefied in the remarks of one airline executive who admitted, “We ordered the big jets as quickly as we could, then asked our engineers and economists to prove that we had made the right decision!”
On October 26, 1958, Pan American’s B-707 jet, “Clipper America”, departed from Idlewild Airport for Paris carrying 111 passengers on the first trans-Atlantic service by a U.S. commercial jet. The airline’s new jets introduced a new simplified livery, featuring the large blue globe on the aircraft vertical fins. Capable of carrying up to 165 economy class passengers, the 707 could do the work of 3 DC-7s!
