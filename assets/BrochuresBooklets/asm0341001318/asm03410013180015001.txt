CHAPTER II - THE “POST WAR” YEARS
Although Pan American’s pioneering spirit never waned, it was severly checked by post-war administrations in the U.S. which took a less favorable view of the airline’s “grandfather rights.’’ Aspiring entrants upon the international field at the close of the war,- most of whom had had the “taste of honey’’ in ATC and NATS flying during the war,- pointed to Pan American’s “monopolistic” situation, and argued the need for “healthy competition” on the world air routes which had been so hard won by Pan American in those pre-war years.
In June, 1944, the C.A.B. announced that it would receive applications for international route certificates from interested parties. Of course there were many “interested parties,” since so many U.S. airlines (22 in all) had gained experience in international flying during the war under contract to ATC and NATS. Out of these cases, in 1946, came awards to competing airlines for routes to Latin America, to the Far East and across the Atlantic to Europe.
On the post-war international scene was a world-wide string of land airports, built during the war to serve the military needs. These airfields were capable of accommodating the initial post-war generations of 4-engined land planes.
Douglas DC-4, Miami Beach
In 1945 Pan American resumed trans-Atlantic service with B-314 flying boats until the company could take delivery of an initial batch of war surplus C-54s and convert them to passenger carrying DC-4s.
On October 27, 1945, Pan American started DC-4 service on the New York - London route.
In January, 1946, the DC-4s were put on the Miami - San Juan run; and by mid 1946 the DC-4s were operating throughout Latin America. Flying boat operations were shut down around the end of 1945 at Miami; and in January, 1946, the Dinner Key base was closed! Today, incidentally, the terminal building at Dinner Key is owned by the City of Miami; and the base is a public marina for pleasure craft.
In January, 1946, Pan American took delivery of its first Lockheed Constellations. First regular service for this type was between New York and Bermuda. The Connies were introduced on the New York - Lisbon service January 20th, and to London a couple of weeks later on February 3rd, 1946.

Replacing the B-314s on the Honolulu to San Francisco run in April, 1946, the L-049 Constellations cut the flying time from 17 1/2 hours to under ten!
Pan American had started operations back in 1927 with the best equipment then available -the Fokker F-7s which carried Wright engines of 220 HP! Nineteen years later, in 1946, the high-flying “Connies” came into service capable of three times the speed and mounting Wright engines of just 10 times the power -2200 HP! A total of 856 “Connies” were turned out at Lockheed in twelve years of production. Successive models featured stretched fuselages and wings, engine power increases to 3,400 HP and gross weight increases from the original 90,000 lbs. to a final 160,000 lbs.!
