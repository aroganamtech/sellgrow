A dark business suit, as opposed to formal attire, is the standard for most evening social functions. As with most things American, dress is inclined to be somewhat informal. The fact is, in the privacy of their offices, many executives prefer to work jacket-less, in shirtsleeves. If discussions get lengthy or involved, chances are you may be invited to remove your coat for comfort.
CURRENCY IN THE UNITED STATES
American money consists of the following coins: 10 (penny), 50 (nickel), 100 (dime), 250 (quarter), 500 (half-dollar). Silver dollars are rare in the East but common in the West. Paper money is in denominations of $1, $2, $5, $10, $20, $50, $100 and up to $10,000. The $2 bill is often thought to be "unlucky," and some people refuse to handle them.
There is no restriction on the importation of foreign currency. Best idea is to obtain small amounts of U.S. dollars before you leave home. But if this is inconvenient, you should make money exchanges immediately upon arrival and carry cash in the form of travelers checks. These are acceptable anywhere, and leftover checks can be readily changed back into your own currency when you get home.
TIPPING
Service in the United States varies from excellent to poor, but you will be expected to tip no matter what your estimation of the quality. Americans have grown up with the idea of tipping for services good or bad; it is an accepted part of life. Don't be too surprised — should you fail to tip a waiter because service is poor —to see him express his displeasure with a dark look or a few mumbled words of discontent.
In taxicabs, tip the driver about 150 for a short ride, 200 when the meter reads from 600 to $1, about 15% of the total fare for long rides. Generally 15%
