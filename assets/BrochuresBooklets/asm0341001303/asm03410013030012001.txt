PAN AMERICAN CLIPPERS —
FIRST ACROSS THE ATLANTIC
For all airlines, the Atlantic was the greatest challenge, and the greatest goal. The Atlantic offered no series of islands as convenient service bases. Practical routes had to be found. And no airplane so far developed had the reach for the non-stop stretches imposed by the Atlantic.
Pan American started its route exploration early. Even as Pan Am was developing its flight courses in South America, initial development work was started on transatlantic. Research expeditions probed climate and meteorological conditions in Iceland, Greenland, Labrador and Newfoundland. Engineers took readings on the Azores and Africa, looking toward a mid-Atlantic route. Air surveys were made of all possible routes, including the Far North. Out of this study two dependable routes emerged — one a Great Circle to the north, the other mid-Atlantic through the Azores.
To fly the Atlantic, Pan American needed a Super-Clipper. Pan Am engineers drew up the specifications. Out of this came the Boeing 314 — a truly great ship, mistress of the air for the world’s most challenging ocean.
On May 20, 1939 the “Yankee Clipper” gathered speed in the waters of Port Washington, Long Island. Her destination Lisbon — then on to Marseilles. On May 27, she returned to home waters, and the first scheduled air mail service across the Atlantic was completed. On June 24, a Pan American Clipper made the first flight via the northern Great Circle route to England — and on June 28 the “Dixie Clipper” carried the first air passengers in history across the Atlantic.
Pan American now had more than 60,000 miles of routes. It also had 5,000,000 miles of over-water flying experience behind it. The great land areas of the globe had now been joined. For Pan Am, for the world, this was the beginning of a new era.
