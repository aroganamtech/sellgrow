PAN AMERICAN JOINS THE AMERICAS
In 1929 it first became clear that Pan American was more than a private enterprise undergoing lusty growth. It was establishing the United States in the world of . international air, knitting together far-separated countries with its thread of routes.
While expanding in the Caribbean, Pan Am also acquired routes in Mexico, with service from Brownsville, Texas. In this same period Pan American, in partnership with W. R. Grace & Co., established air service down the West Coast of South America — Panama to Santiago, Chile and over the Andes to Buenos Aires.
Once again, to match flying equipment to the longer flight stretches, Pan American and Sikorsky developed the S-40, the first American 4-engined flying boat, and the first proud airship to carry the name Clipper. This was an impressive aircraft — a true forerunner of the magnificent Clipper fleets to come. It carried 40 passengers, had a cruising speed of 100 miles-per-hour, and a range of 800 miles.
By the end of 1929 Pan American had grown from the original 90-mile spur from Key West to Cuba to a system totaling 12,265 route miles. It had a fleet of 64 aircraft. It had carried 15,000 passengers and 530,000 pounds of mail in 1928 alone. It had become the first U. S. airline to employ instrument flying and direction-finding techniques. And in 1930 Pan American completed the encirclement of South America by acquiring routes up the East Coast — thus filling in the final line in a giant figure 8 joining the Americas.
