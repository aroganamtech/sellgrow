JL he Caribbean sea and the West Indies, in Pan American’s early years, provided an immense operational testing laboratory.
This area of ocean expanse, dotted with large and small islands, some close together and some hundreds of miles apart was ideal for this purpose. Here on a condensed scale, were all the problems that an aspiring world air operator would meet.
In stretching its wings beyond Cuba, Pan American learned the logistics of growth, gained fast experience in overseas operation.
New routes to the south were surveyed, then put into operation: to Nassau, down the West Indies chain to Puerto Rico, and beyond.
Further extension presented problems of land airport sites. Plainly, an amphibious marine transport was needed, a plane that could operate in land airports, or “make its own airport” on water. At this point, Pan American established its permanent policy of developing its own specifications for aircraft. Out of this came the Sikorsky S-38, a pioneer twin-engine amphibian — a plane that could carry 8 passengers, cruise at 105 miles per hour, for a distance of 300 miles.
Now practically any point in the Caribbean could be a port of call. Pan Am’s routes spread westward from Cuba to Yucatan, then south through Central America to the Canal Zone. Another arm extended from Cuba down the backbone of the Greater and Lesser Antilles, as far south as Dutch Guiana. Further survey flights closed the connection between these two fingers, and Pan American became a system in the true sense of the word.
