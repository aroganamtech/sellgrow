FOKKER F-7
In 1927, the year of its birth, Pan American began flying mail and passengers over a 90-mile route between Key West, Florida and Havana, Cuba.
Since there were no seaplanes in the United States that filled the requirements of this flight, a land plane was chosen. It was the FOKKER F-7, powered by three Wright 220-horsepower engines. The F-7 cruised at 85 miles-an-hour, a respectable speed in those days. Its wing was made of plywood. It carried 10 passengers, if none brought luggage along.
SIKORSKY S-42
By 1934, Pan Am was ready to cross the oceans. In that year, the SIKORSKY S-42 joined the fleet. This was America’s first true ocean transport. Built to rigid specifications which required a plane to fly 2,400 miles with a safe fuel reserve, and operate with one engine out of commission, the S-42 broke all performance records for flying boats. It weighed 38,000 pounds and could carry 32 passengers. Among other innovations, the S-42 used flush riveting to reduce air drag, wing “flaps” to reduce landing speed, and constant-speed propellers. The S-42 was assigned to regular service in the Caribbean and on such routes as New York-Bermuda. As the American Clipper, an S-42 surveyed the routes across the Pacific and over the Atlantic to Europe.
[ARTIN M-130, THE CHINA CLIPPER
The CHINA CLIPPER . . . The MARTIN M-130 . . . was the plane chosen to make the pioneering dream come true. The first scheduled air mail flight across the Pacific left California on November 22, 1935. After 59 hours and 48 minutes of flying time (six days’ elapsed time) she sat down in Manila Harbor!
Largest air transport of its time, the M-130 cruised 3,200 miles non-stop at 130 miles-an-hour, and carried up to 32 passengers, depending on the pay-load of mail and freight. By present-day standards, the M-130 is small, but at the time, it was immense. It weighed 52,000 pounds, or 26 tons, had a wing span of 130 feet, stood 24 feet high, and was 90 feet, 7 inches long. It had four Pratt and Whitney 900-horsepower engines.
