The jets you see in Hangar 14 are the very latest being flown by any airline. But the pace of the aviation industry is such that they will soon be outdated. Already, Pan Am is hard at work helping to design supersonic jets that will whisk passengers from New York to London in 2V2 hours and less.
In 1963, Pan Am ordered six Concorde supersonic jets, which will be built by Sud Aviation of France, under a joint agreement with Britain, and with the help of PAA engineers. The prototype of the Concorde is expected to be in the air by the summer of 1966 —and the first production models should be flying passengers by 1968. These planes will carry a hundred people and their baggage at speeds of 1,500 mph.
But Pan Am engineers have their eyes even farther on the future —on a. day not far off when even the Concorde will be considered “slow.” The Concorde, you see, will have a “skin” of duraluminum — the same material that covers today’s jets. It cannot stand the tremendous heat generated by the friction of air rushing past a plane at speeds above 1,500 mph.
As a result, our engineers and others suggested that the United States government sponsor a design competition for a plane built of a material that would not have a “ top speed.” Steel and titanium were suggested as materials that could beat the friction-heat problem. Another idea advanced to aid speed: movable wings, like a bird’s.
