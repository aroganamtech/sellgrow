BOEING B-314, THE DIXIE CLIPPER
The BOEING B-314, PAN AM’S DIXIE CLIPPER, was the last and largest of the seaplane fleet. It inaugurated the first scheduled service across the Atlantic in 1939. Weighing 42 tons, the Dixie Clipper had a wing span of 152 feet, and a length of 106 feet. The four Wright engines delivered 1,500-horsepower each. The engines were accessible to the Flight Engineer for repair while in flight. The B-314'showed that flying had come of age from the standpoint of comfort, luxury and utility. Like an ocean liner, it had staterooms . . . including a bridal suite . . . and passengers could relax in over-stuffed chairs and sofas. It could carry 74 passengers. There were nine B-314’s in Pan Am’s fleet.
DOUGLAS DC-4
Having made its contribution to the war effort, Pan Am, in 1945, quickly got back into civilian dress. Planning for post-war progress brought into the Clipper fleet a whole parade of planes. The DOUGLAS DC-4, whose reliability and versatility were proven by its brilliant war record, could cruise at up to 260 miles-an-hour with a payload of 55 passengers in first class service, or 70 passengers in tourist service, as well as 8,000 pounds of cargo. It could also provide Sleeperette accommodations for 30 passengers. The DC-4 had four Pratt and Whitney 1,450-horsepower engines. Its operating range was 3,500 miles. Wing span was 117 feet, 6 inches, and its length was 93 feet, 10 inches.
Another plane of this era was the Lockheed Constellation, with which Pan Anx began aviation’s first scheduled round-the-world flights in 1947.
BOEING 377 STRATOCLIPPER
In 1949, Pan Am took a giant step forward with the Boeing 377 Stratocruiser, called Stratoclipfier by Pan Am. The double-decked B-377, the heaviest piston-engine commercial aircraft ever built, had a maximum takeoff weight of 147,000 pounds. The lounge on the lower deck was linked to the passenger cabin by a spiral staircase. The Stratoclippers were fitted with comfortable bunks, staterooms, and large, comfortable seats. In December, 1960, when Pan Am retired its B-377’s, each had been in the air over 30,000 hours. The B-377 had a wing span of 141 feet, three inches; was 110 feet, four inches long; and had a tail that stood four stories high—38 feet, three inches.
