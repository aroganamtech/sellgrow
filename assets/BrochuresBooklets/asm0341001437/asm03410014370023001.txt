Those Men Twisting the Propellers
You noticed the men turning the propellers before takeoff? Well, in the early days of aircraft they might have been starting the engines. But we have electric starters now. You might say they were “loosening up” the engines, much in the way an athlete flexes his muscles before exerting effort.
The "Run Up"
At the takeoff end of the runway, you’ll hear the engines roar up to full power one by one and the ship will quiver slightly as though eager to be off. This is known as a “run up” and the pilot does it to one engine after another to make certain each power plant is in perfect operating condition.
Hold Everything
The engines have been tested. We’re all ready to go. So why don’t we?
In all probability it’s what’s known as a “hold”. Perhaps the airport control tower has instructed your Captain to wait for an incoming plane. (They have priority.) In a few moments the runway will be clear—the tower will give your Captain the “green light”, and off you’ll go. (Bet you never notice when the wheels leave the ground.) “Holds” sometimes take place before you land, too . . . usually when planes are arriving faster than airport landing facilities can accommodate them. The planes are assigned certain altitude levels at which to circle. This is known as “stacking”. As soon as the lowest plane has landed, all the rest drop down one level, and so on, until all have been brought in.
What Is That Noise?
If you hear a strange sound from the engines directly after landing and feel a slight jolt, you’ll know the pilot has reversed the propellers, to slow your Clipper’s taxiing speed. A “braking” system, you might say.
