The keynote of this war has been SPEED ... It began as a "Blitzkrieg” (lightning-war) on Poland. And there is no defense against speed except more speed.
That is why the Clippers have played such an important part in flying overseas the military leaders of the United Nations. Letters are slow, subject to loss, interception. Radio-telephones can be listened in on by the enemy, cables cut.
But on wings, leaders get together fast! They meet, plan and straighten out misunderstandings face to face. For such meetings (as these two pages show) the Clippers, operating over 82,000 miles of world routes even with a war going on, have provided speedy, dependable over-ocean transport. . . . To Alaska, to Hawaii and Australasia ... To England, to Latin America, to Lisbon, to Africa and on the over-ocean part of the highly important Casablanca Conference.

The date is January 30, 1942 (which ivas the President's 61st birthday). Left to right, on their way home from Casablanca, Chief-of-staff Admiral Leahy, President Roosevelt, Harry Hopkins, and PAA Captain H. M. Cone.
Since War began, trained PAA Flight Crews have flown many a plane beside the Clippers. To one such crew was assigned the honor of flying General Dwight D. Eisenhower, Supreme Commander of the Allied Expeditionary Forces.
Major General Harold L. George . . . He started ivith little—has built up the great Army Air Forces Air Transport Command. For the ATC, Pan American Flight Crews have flown more than 22,500,000 plane miles on war assignments . . . Among their passengers has been General George himself.
