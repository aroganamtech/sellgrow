The British government has made it clear exactly how things stand.
Here’s What the British say about Heathrow-Gatwick
On October 9, 1979, John Nott, British Secretary of State for Trade, declared:
“That the practice, which has been followed since April 1977, under which no airline which is not presently operating scheduled international passenger services to Heathrow is allowed to start services at Heathrow, is confirmed as Government policy.”
On the same date, Norman Payne, Chairman of the British Airports Authority, said:
“We welcome the Government’s policy that no further airlines will be allowed to start services from Heathrow.”
John Mulkern, Managing Director of the British Civil Aviation Authority, commenting on Eastern Air Lines, one of the announced contenders for the Miami-London route, told some 70 airline officials on October 5:
“Eastern, if it gets the route, will go to Gatwick.”
That seems about as clear as it can possibly be. If a different airline is assigned the route, the British will consider it a new carrier. And British policy clearly and firmly is that new carriers fly to Gatwick.
Right now, Florida is guaranteed access to London’s premier airport and the most advantageous connections for beyond-London service. If Pan Am and National combined keep the route, that guarantee holds, and Florida comes out ahead.
