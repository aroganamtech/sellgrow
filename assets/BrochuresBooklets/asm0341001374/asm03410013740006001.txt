Half the fun of traveling is acquiring duty-free “bargains.” And your purchases up to the value of $200, $300 or even $500 — depending upon the circumstances — are exempt from duty if (1) they are for your personal or household use; (2) you declare them properly on your arrival in the United States; (3) your trip was not made just to buy them; (4) you didn’t order them ahead of time; (5) you didn’t send them from abroad as gifts.
The $200 exemption, under which you may bring back liquor or cigars, is always applied before the $300 exemption. Ordinarily, you have to be outside the country for at least 48 hours to qualify for it. However, if you return through a port along the California-Mexico border, you may use the $200 exemption after only a 24-hour absence. There is no time restriction at all on articles acquired in Mexico if you return through a port in Texas, Arizona or New Mexico. But in no case is this exemption allowable more often than once in any 31-day period.
You get the additional $300 exemption only if you have been abroad at least 12 days and have not claimed it within 6 months prior to your return from this trip.
Your exemptions may include the cost of alterations and of dutiable repairs on anything you took abroad — such as your car, radio, watch, etc. But remember this: Your exemptions are lost on any article which you fail to declare on your return from the trip on which you acquired it. And if you sell within three years any article passed duty-free under the $300 exemption, you will be penalized double the duty otherwise collectible. The time periods, amount of exemption, and other requirements are rigidly enforced. No customs officer can make an exception for you.
