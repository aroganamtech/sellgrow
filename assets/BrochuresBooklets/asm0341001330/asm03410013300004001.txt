History has a way of being repetitive. Within less than a century, the struggle for commerce was repeated — this time, for commerce in the air. On October 12, 1931, Mrs. Herbert Hoover, wife of the President of the United States, bestowed for the first time on an American aircraft, the title AMERICAN CLIPPER. Both significant and apt was this name, for, with the oldtime Yankee determination, America’s young international airline Pan American World Airways was facing well-entrenched competition from foreign airlines. In just such a struggle, Yankee shipbuilders had come up with the Clipper ships, and now Pan American introduced a new Clipper — the world’s largest Marine aircraft.
It all started — the magnificent Clipper Ship era — with Isaac McKim, a wealthy Baltimore merchant, who, in 1832, commissioned the building of the first Clipper ship and named her the Ann McKim in honor of his wife. This ship was the forerunner of all those elegant, high-masted symbols of American maritime might. So beautiful, so efficient, these ships became a legend in their own time. The ports of China, of Australia, of Africa and California knew them, as did the wild winds of the China Sea, Cape Horn, the Pacific and the Atlantic. In pursuit of trade, their beauty burst upon the world — long black hulls, knife-sharp bows and thousands of yards of canvas, billowing and flapping aloft.
What made the Clippers a living legend?
Clippers had class. They had smooth seams, polished black hulls, holystoned decks that gleamed a cream-white. One even had a library that cost over $1200, an extravagant sum in those days when money could have been better spent on rigging and sail! Guilded pilasters, elegant wainscoting of satinwood, mahogany and rosewood made up the passenger quarters. And the masts! Taller than the stacks of today’s great transoceanic liners.
