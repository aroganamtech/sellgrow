PAN AM BUILDING
Emblem of our time. Symbol of cooperation between nations. Working place for thousands. Monument of the free enterprise system. This is the Pan Am Building. It derives its name from Pan American Airways, the major occupant of the structure. Pan Am, the world’s most experienced airline, uses more than 600,000 square feet of space.
Construction of the Pan Am Building began in June, 1960; and it was ready for initial occupancy in March, 1962. It is the world’s largest commercial office building with 2,400,000 square feet of office space—equal to a 60 acre tract, if spread out on a level— on a 31/2 acre plot of mid-Manhattan real estate.
The Pan Am Building was constructed over 24 operating railroad tracks above Grand Central Station without disrupting scheduled train service—a major engineering feat. Because of the railroad tracks below it, the building has no basement. All of its ventilating and heating facilities are located above the ground.
A remarkable circular traffic pattern enables cars to drive directly into the building as they come north or south on the Park Avenue overpass and park in a 400 car garage for a nominal fee. From there, people may walk directly into the main lobby of the building.

Standing 808 feet tall, the Pan Am Building has 59 floors, 8,000 windows and a spacious roof-top which serves as New York’s newest heliport. From this dramatic site, frequent scheduled helicopter service carries passengers to and from Kennedy International Airport and Teterboro Airport, New Jersey.
Operation by New York Airways started on December 21, 1965. The trip between the Pan Am Building in Midtown Manhattan and the Pan Am Departure Terminal at Kennedy Airport takes only a few minutes, and an average of 300 people make the trip daily.
On the 58th floor is the ’Copter Club. Excellent food and generous drinks are served amid a beautiful decor — and with a spectacular view of New York.
With approximately 17,000 people working with companies from Japan, Italy, Spain, France, England, Canada, Jamaica, Mexico, South America and the United States, the Pan Am Building is truly an international structure. It has been estimated that an average of 250,000 people from all walks of life enter the building daily. Many of them use the majestic 84 foot wide entrance on Vanderbilt Avenue, the 76 foot entrance at East 45th Street, or the escalators rising from the main lobby of Grand Central Terminal.
The first in-building central telephone communications center serves 20,000 telephones in the Pan Am Building and in nearby offices —as many telephones as in a small city. Cost of the installation was $11,000,000. 1,000 tons of equipment and 50,000 miles of wire and cable were needed.
120,000 kilowatt hours of electricity are consumed daily—enough electric power to supply 10,000 homes.
This edifice of electronic equipment, stone, and masonry is the international headquarters of Pan American Airways, whose swift Jet Clippers®connect New York City with 121 cities in 84 lands on all six continents.

The fourth floor of the Pan Am Building houses PANAMAC,® the fastest, most reliable Passenger/Cargo reservations and information system in service today. Created by IBM for Pan American at a cost of 23 million dollars, PANAMAC uses the very latest advances in computer and communications technology. In seconds, PANAMAC electronically scans records and schedules, makes all arrangements for travel anywhere around the world. Car rentals, taxi service, tours, and hotel reservations at any of the 35 Intercontinental Hotels and other fine hotels worldwide are instantly arranged. PANAMAC also improves cargo services, helps to cut the time needed to process shipments, and maintains an up to the minute cargo space availability record for all Pan Am flights everywhere.
