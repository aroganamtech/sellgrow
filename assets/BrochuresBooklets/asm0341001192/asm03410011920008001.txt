Clothes: A “heat wave” in England seldom means over 80 degrees and Scotland is even cooler so don’t take American summer clothes unless you’re going on to warmer countries. Winters require warm clothing but are not nearly so cold, even in Scotland, as in the northern U.S. because the sea is never far away. A raincoat and umbrella are useful the year round, and boots, so long as they’re fashionable, are ideal for Britain’s constantly unpredictable weather. Otherwise, city shoes should combine smartness with low-heeled comfort; tough, sturdy clompers are a British tradition for walking in the country. Even in the best hotels, heat is turned off by the calendar instead of the thermometer and a warmish bathrobe is always recommended. In winter, most hotels and just about all homes are heated much less warmly than in the States. A cocktail dress and business suit are adequate for most parties, but take formal clothes (and warm wraps for ladies) if you expect any special social life, even in the country, which is otherwise staunchly tweeds-and-sweaters.
Currency: The British pound is now divided into 100 new pence, instead of 20 shillings of 12 pence each. Thus there are 100 new pence in one pound (£.), which is valued at U.S.$2.40. The new 5p coin replaces the shilling (120), and the 50p coin replaces the 10 shilling note ($1.20). The sixpence (6d) coin, which is equivalent to 2Vi new pence (60), remains as legal tender. See currency chart, page 64.
Electricity: Newer hotels have special outlets for U.S. razors, but the current (220-250 volts, 50 cycles) and wall outlets (non-screw type) require adapter plugs and voltage converters.
