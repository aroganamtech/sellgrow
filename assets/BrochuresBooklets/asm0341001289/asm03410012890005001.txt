The plane that made its own airport
When, in 1929, Pan Am extended its service across the Caribbean to the north coast of South America, a new kind of passenger plane was needed
... one that could fly beyond the range of existing commercial aircraft... and that could land in areas without prepared airfields.
The twin-engine Sikorsky S-38 amphibian filled the bill admirably. Its two 425-horsepower engines kept the S-38's 10,480 pounds barrelling along at 105 miles per hour, with a range of 300 miles. With its added range, the S-38 helped Pan Am expand its route coverage to more than 11,000 miles.
The Caribbean — Pan Am's testing lab
When Pan Am stretched its wings beyond Cuba, we found — in the Caribbean — an oceanful of new aviation problems.
For instance, over-water crossings were longer than ever before. New routes had to be surveyed and put into operation — to Nassau, to Puerto Rico .. . and beyond.
In pushing further south, Pan Am had to overcome a major obstacle — landing sites. Often there weren't any. Plainly, an amphibious marine transport was needed — one that could make a safe landing on water or land.
As you saw, a highly successful answer was developed — the Sikorsky amphibian. At the same time, we established a
permanent policy: Pan Am planes were to be designed to Pan Am specifications.
Now, practically any point in the Caribbean could be a port of call. Pan Am's routes spread westward from Cuba to Yucatan, then south through Central America to the Canal Zone. Another arm extended from Cuba down the backbone of the Greater and Lesser Antilles, as far south as Guiana. Further survey flights closed the connection between these two fingers, and Pan American became a system in the true sense of the word.
