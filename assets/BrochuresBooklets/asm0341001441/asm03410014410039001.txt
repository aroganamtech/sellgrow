Air Transport operations to Alaska following the war assumed an entirely different character from the pre-war situation. Then, in less than a decade and a half, Pan American had done all the pioneering necessary to opening Alaska for air commerce.
Airports existed, where there were none before.
Radio facilities were available - Pan American put them in.
Facilities for repair and maintenance of aircraft were on hand. Before Pan American, such facilities were meager at best, and usually non-existent.
With the pioneering finished, the post-war progress of the company assumed the trend of refinements, rather than major developments of new routes. Service was improved, schedules adapted to the convenience of steadily increasing numbers of travelers.
Fares were reduced as traffic volume increased - a reflection of Pan American's basic policy of making mass transportation by air available at prices an ordinary citizen can afford.
Introduction of the new, 300-mile an hour DC-6B Clippers was merely an extension of this policy: Carry more people farther and faster in greater comfort, and the laws of supply and demand will function to the ultimate benefit of the consumer.
In mid-september, 1953, Pan American carried its policy of lowest possible fares one step farther on Alaskan routes and slashed rates to the lowest minimum permitted by the CAB. Result was the lowest round-trip rates offered by anyone serving Alaska.
Cuts in round-trip fares between Seattle-Portland and Alaskan cities ranged up to 10 per cent. For instance between Seattle and Nome the round-trip fares were reduced from $230 to $207. The Seattle-Juneau round-trip was set at $99, down from $110.
