Flying in Alaska at that time was pretty disorganized by ’’Outside” standards. There were none of the scheduled operations and efficient air carrier methods which Pan American, as the first international airline, had helped to establish. Service to the public was on what amounted to a barnstorming basis. Satisfactory airports just didn't exist, nor did the navigational aids which were already commonplace in the continental U.S. The terrain was rugged and lacked emergency landing fields. Temperatures ranged from 80 above to 60 below zero and electrical and magnetic disturbances made radio communication a nightmare.
It was a difficult and expensive challenge. What PAA set out to do was to conduct on a long-range basis a sort of an Arctic "laboratory” in which to accumulate the experience necessary for (1) - re-establishment of local services on the most efficient basis; (2) - extension of routes from Alaska to the U.S.; and (3) -ultimate extension to the Orient over the Arctic route when technical and political obstacles could be overcome.
The years 1932-33 marked the first phase of this program - a sort of a "shakedown" period, which included acquisition of newer aircraft, erection of a modern hangar and maintenance shop at Fairbanks, and steps toward establishment of scheduled operations on basic trunk routes. Initially, more Fairchild 71s were purchased, to be followed by Consolidated Fleetsters.
