In 1939 Pan Am turned to the Atlantic. On May 20, the Boeing 314 flying boat "Yankee Clipper" took off from Port Washington, New York. This airmail flight, after a stop at Horta, Azores, landed in Lisbon 26 hours and 54 minutes later, and the next day it continued on to Marseilles. On June 24 airmail service began to Southampton, England from Port Washington via Shediac, New Brunswick, Botwood, Newfoundland, and Foynes, Eire. The first transatlantic passenger flight took place on June 28 when the "Dixie Clipper" flew from Port Washington to Lisbon and Marseilles, and passenger service started to England on July 8, 1939.
Then came World War II. The airline served its government, not only flying priority personnel and cargo, but teaching military pilots the long-range over-water flying and navigation techniques it had developed, by providing the military know-how it had amassed in building stepping-stone air bases across oceans and continents, and by actually operating for the military an air service from the U.S. via South America, the South Atlantic, and across North Africa.
After the war, Pan Am resumed peacetime travel expansion. It turned to commercial use aircraft developed during the war, like the Douglas DC-4, the first ocean-crossing land plane, the Lockheed Constellation, the Boeing Stratocruiser, the Douglas DC-6 and Douglas DC-7.
It extended routes even further, and in 1947 the Lockheed Constellation "Clipper America" inaugurated round-the-world commercial air service on June 17.
This inaugural flight went from New York to Gander, Shannon, London, Istanbul, Dhahran, Karachi, Calcutta, Bangkok, Manila, Shanghai, Tokyo, Guam, Wake, Midway, Honolulu, San Francisco, Chicago, arriving back in New York on June 30.
