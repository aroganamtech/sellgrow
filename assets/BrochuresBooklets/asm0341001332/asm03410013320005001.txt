The "new Pan Am," combining the strengths of Pan American World Airways and National Airlines, officially took off at 5 p.m. on January 7, 1980, when Pan Am became the owner of 100 percent of the stock of National.
The merger created a new worldwide airline with an extensive U.S. domestic route system, international routes linking the United States with major foreign cities on six continents, a large fleet of modern jet aircraft and human resources second to none in the airline industry.
The creation of the new Pan Am marks the start of a new era in the company's history. It is a time of new opportunity and new commitment for everyone associated with the new airline.
Pan Am had long wanted routes within the United States that would feed passengers into its international route system. Through the years, however,
Pan Am's applications for these routes had been denied by the federal government. Yet other U.S. carriers with extensive domestic route systems were permitted to expand into international markets served by Pan Am. At the same time, the government gave foreign-flag carriers increased access to U.S. markets, and they all, of course, had domestic and regional systems abroad.
Pan Am was the only major international airline in the world without a domestic network to carry passengers to its foreign-bound flights, a lack that was becoming an increasingly serious competitive handicap.
The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 would probably have permitted Pan Am to develop eventually a domestic route system. By this time, however, it was no longer feasible from either a time or cost standpoint. The only alternative was for Pan Am to merge with a domestic airline that had a traffic base capable of supporting international operations.
