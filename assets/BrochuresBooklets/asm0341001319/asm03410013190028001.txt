Beyond the many obvious benefits—such as roominess that permits passengers to wander around during flight, three different movies projected simultaneously, an upper deck bar and lounge in the first class section that converts into a dining room at mealtime—the 747 has scores of engineering features that make it extraordinarily safe. For example, its inertial navigation system (the same kind that guided the Apollo space flights) has not just one but two complete back-up systems. Maintenance people refer to this as "redundancy”—more than will ever be needed, but there anyhow. There are dozens of other examples, including two auto-pilots
instead of the usual one, and double hydraulic systems. The 747 also has advanced radar with a 300-mile range and an automatic electronic landing system that uses computers to bring the aircraft safely to a runway in even the worst weather. Engines are 30 percent more fuel efficient than those in the original 707s and operate at lower noise levels. Pilots and maintenance crews refer to the 747 as an "honest” airplane, because it seldom surprises them. Its instruments tell them far ahead of time when it may need attention.
The passenger space of the 747 is impressive —but even more so is the tremendous amount of cargo which Pan Am’s 747 jet freighters
can carry: approximately 100 tons, more than twice the capacity of a 707, and it can transport many bulky items which formerly could not go by air—such as printing presses, deep sea diving submarines used for oil exploration, and luxury limousines. And with mechanized handling of containerized and palletized cargo (pioneered by Pan Am) a fully loaded 747 can be unloaded in less than three hours. Shipping by air on Pan Am is now competitive with surface shipment, and the volume of cargo carried by Pan Am has doubled since 1966.
