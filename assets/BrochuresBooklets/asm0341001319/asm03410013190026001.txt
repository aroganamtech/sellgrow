The reduction in luxury was, for most travelers, more than offset by the savings. The round trip fare from New York to London was now $436, compared to the former $567 tourist class fare and the $783 first class rate.
During the first three months of 1959 Pan Am’s 707s carried 33,400 passengers—a good measure of the popularity of the new jets, as well as their capacity. Actually, one 707 could do as much work as three DC-7s, largest of the piston-engine airliners. (A figure-minded Pan Am employee also determined that a single 707, making one round trip from New York to London every 24 hours, could carry as many passengers as the Queen Mary!)
To handle the increased volume Pan Am instituted the first computerized reservations system. Called Panamac, it handled Pan Am reservations worldwide, and became the model for all other airlines.
By the end of 196*2 Pan Am was operating some 60 707s and DC-8s, and very few piston-engine planes were still flying the long routes.
In 1963 Pan Am inaugurated the first allcargo jet flights—again, ahead of the rest of the industry. Capable of carrying over 76,000 pounds, these swift cargo carriers could also transport the kind of perishable items that formerly could not have been shipped at all.
