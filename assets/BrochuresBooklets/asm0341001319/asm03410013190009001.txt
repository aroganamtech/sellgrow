And it would be quite a feat to lose yourself with four master mariners aboard and three to five radio compass stations watching every move.” Call it professionalism or supreme self-confidence — that Pan Am crew had it.
During Pan Am’s first months of operation over the Pacific its three Martin flying boats carried only mail and cargo on a once-a-week schedule in each direction. In October, 1936, passenger service began—and over 1,100 requests were received for the seven available seats on the first flight. By this time small, comfortable hotels had been built on each one of the stopover islands.
In 1937 Pan Am celebrated its 10th anniversary. Its routes now covered 50,000 miles, including an expansion of its Pacific service to Macao and Hong Kong and a new route from New York to Bermuda. It had 4,700 employees and 144 aircraft, including the new 180-mph DC-3s.
More important, Pan Am that year carried more than 200,000 passengers. Increasingly, people were not only flying instead of traveling by other means, they were taking trips that a few years earlier they wouldn’t have taken at all. The dream of low-cost air travel for everyone was getting close to being realized.
It was about to get a lot closer.
