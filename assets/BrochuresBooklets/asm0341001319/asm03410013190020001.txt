Pan Am survey teams spread out around the world, inspecting the war-ravaged areas, making plans to rebuild and re-equip overseas bases and get them in shape for the resumption of normal schedules. One team left San Francisco right after the Japanese surrender and made 27 stops in 28 days, covering the entire South Pacific and Orient. And everywhere they went they were welcomed with open arms. An old friend was back in business.
The war had changed many things, but Pan Am’s goal to bring foreign travel within the reach of everyone was the same. Juan Trippe renewed that pledge: "Air transport has the very clear choice of becoming a luxury service to carry the well-to-do at high prices—or to carry the average man at what he can afford to pay. Pan American has chosen the latter course.”
A hit song of the mid-40’s was "Don’t Fence Me In”—and that’s the way people felt. They were in the mood to travel. Restricted during the long years of war, they were ready to cut loose, to finally get that trip to Paris or Rio or Bermuda or Honolulu off the ground. And Pan Am was getting ready to help them do it.
