We don’t start immediately. First the Captain and his crew members check all the dials on the instrument panels to be sure everything is functioning correctly.
We’re moving now, which means the Captain was satisfied with his check of the instruments. We pull away from the loading apron, proceed along a taxi strip, and stop at the end of the runway.
Once again there is a pause for a final check of the instruments, and the flight could be delayed if any reading should be unsatisfactory. However, the Captain again is satisfied. The sound of our jet engines raises in volume, we start to move down the runway, slowly at first, then faster, and after a run rather longer than would be taken by the older piston engine aircraft, we rise into the air . . . We are airborne now. The airport buildings, cars and airplanes get smaller until they look like toys.
Now we proceed on a course over the ocean, eastward, paralleling Long Island, and we continue to climb until we are flying at 35,000 feet.
Even at 30,000 feet, the air pressure inside the cabin is set at 5,000 feet, making it comfortable for everyone.
We’re en route to Paris, on the first section of our Round the World flight.
