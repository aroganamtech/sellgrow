American kept its head, drawing on reserves of knowledge and experience to guide its judgement. BOAC’s Comet 1 had less range than the pre-war Boeing 314 and, when the American ai rli ne made its jet decision, on October 20, 1952, it chose the Comet 3, with trans-Atlantic range, and, curiously, delivery position ahead of BOAC.
The early 1950s was a period of uncertainty. The Comet success was halted dramatically by the Mediterranean tragedies early in 1954. The Boeing 367-80, the 707 prototype, made its first flight on July 15 of the same year. Pan American’s final decision had repercussions throughout the airline world. The order for 42 “ Big Jet ” aircraft, 21 Boeing 707s and 21 DC-8s, placed on October 13, 1955, started an airline queue to the United States West Coast manufacturers which has not been emulated since.
While the rest of the airline world worked out ways of meeting equipment competition of such magnitude, Pan American went about its business, making, incidentally another, less publicised, decision. It by-passed the turbo-prop, developing instead its piston-engined fleets to their utmost. Standardising on Douglas, it placed the DC-7B on North Atlantic service on June 13, 1955, and the DC-7C on June 1, 1956. The latter aircraft was the first to fly New York-London non-stop on a regular scheduled basis.
Even the advent of BOAC’s turboprop Britannias late in 1957 did little to slow Pan American’s inexorable progress, and the same rival’s Atlantic jet debut with the new Comet 4s on October 4, 1958, was countered almost immediately by Boeing 707s on October 26. Since that date Pan American has augmented its jet fleet to keep pace with all the latest technical developments in fan engines and “ stretched ” versions, ordering adequate numbers as demanded by the traffic.
The airline has demonstrated a firm preference for the Boeing product, and appears destined to stay with the Seattle company for many years to come. Although it has secured leading delivery positions for both Anglo-French and American supersonic airliners (8 Concordes and 15 Boeings), the most significant new order has been for 25 Boeing 747 “ Jumbo Jets ” at a total cost, including spares, of well over $500 million. Pan American has not ordered these aircraft “ off the shelf”. As in the case of so many major airliners during its eventful 40-year history, it has taken an active part in the development of the 747 to match its future needs. Its caution in route planning, leaving nothing to chance, and its meticulous attention to detail in its aircraft introduction programmes appear at times to be somewhat conservative. But this is a luxury which Pan American can afford, because its faith in the continued expansion of air transport, and the significant share of the market which it can secure, reflect an aggressive confidence in its own ability to lead the airline world, and not merely go along with it. Pan American looks many years ahead, assesses accurately the needs, and makes firm and visionary decisions to meet those needs.
Pan American is more than an airline. It is a national institution with a World-wide influence, a carrier of American prestige wherever it flies. Today, in its fortieth year, the prospects for continuing expansion appear as exciting as they did when Juan Trippe grasped the nettle, flew to Havana, and dreamed of an airline that would one day encircle the globe.
