Why does Pan American reauire subsidy?
The vast differences between domestic and international operation have already been emphasized, i.e., labor differential, soft currency, and the absence of a single regulatory agency due to the many sovereign governments involved. In several important situations foreign governments have limited the frequency or capacity of the U.S.-flag carriers’ operations into those countries. No one can predict the future impact of foreign government policies on international airline economics; from time to time a foreign government with a strong national airline may grant direct or indirect subsidies or other financial advantages such as free use of capital, may introduce an excessive volume of service, may offer an uneconomic degree of luxury.
These are the fundamental factors which require U.S.-flag subsidy in differing degrees from time to time.
In addition, the volume of international traffic available on the international routes of U.S.-flag carriers is manifestly much smaller than on the domestic trunkline network; many more Americans travel at home than travel overseas; nowhere else in the world is there a volume of air travel comparable to that in the United States.
Pan American’s heaviest international traffic routes sustain a smaller number of trips per week than domestic carriers operate on their major routes per day. (See Charts Nos. 9 and 10.) On some of Pan American’s routes, which have been authorized primarily because of broad national interest considerations, its frequency is as low as two per week in the face of vigorous foreign competition.
In its operations to Puerto Rico, where Pan American has a strong domestic competitor but where foreign competition is excluded, the traffic volume permits a reasonable frequency of service and no subsidy is currently required or paid.
