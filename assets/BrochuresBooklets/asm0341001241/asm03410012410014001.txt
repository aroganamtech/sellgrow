FOOD, A LA MEXICO
f I ''HERE’S more to Mexican cookery than just the hot tamale or chile con carne in cans, and Los Angeles with its big Mexican population offers the visitor a chance to add some entirely new experiences to his gastronomical repertoire. There are dozens of swanky big places with non-Mexican clientele where the more timid may try Americanized versions of Mexican foods. But the real epicure in search of the authentic will search out Los Monitos on Temple Street, near Hill, or El
Coyote, on La Brea near Beverly Boulevard, or will pick others equally genuine among the hundreds of Spanish places, especially those in sections where Mexicans live or shop.
What bread is to us, the tortilla is to the Mexican. He uses it not only to balance the richer items of his meal, but rolls it cone-wise and uses it as spoon or fork, as well. It looks like a thin griddle cake, but is stiffer in texture. It is made of hand-ground corn, wetted and kneaded into a doughy ball from which tortilla makers (usually old women) pinch out a bit which they first squash between their palms, and then toss irom hand to hand, smacking and slapping it until it is thin and round—about six inches in diameter. On a greaseless griddle this is then fried until almost cooked, then added to a stack to be finish-fried just before it is to be served. There isn’t much more taste to a tortilla than there is to bread or macaroni, but most people like them, especially with the richly flavored other things that make up the Mexican meal.
